    Scoping your course

    A badly scoped course is hard to repair: you notice on the third session, when it is too late to start over. This chapter sets out the three decisions to make before writing a single line. It also doubles as a showcase: every engine component is exercised here at least once.

    🎯 Chapter objectives

    • Describe a target audience by its prerequisites and constraints, not by its degree title.
    • Write a verifiable objective using an observable action verb.
    • Choose a through-line that runs across the course and holds it together.

    1. Why scope before producing

    When you know your subject, the temptation is to open a document and start at the beginning. That is almost always a mistake: you then produce what you know, not what the learner needs.

    Scoping means making three decisions, in order: for whom, to do what, and around what. Each one constrains the next. A defined audience narrows the possible objectives; verifiable objectives dictate the kind of activities; the through-line decides the order of chapters. Skipping one of the three does not save time: it moves the time to the rewrite.

    2. Who is learning, exactly?

    "Master's students" describes nobody. A useful audience profile holds three facts: the prerequisites actually acquired, the time available, and the practical constraints. The third is the one people forget, and often the decisive one: a learner following the course on a commute will not watch a twenty-minute video.

    Write that profile down, at the top of the syllabus. It will settle every difficult decision later, and it will stop you writing for the ideal learner, who does not exist.

    3. Objectives you can verify

    A learning objective states what the learner will be able to do, not what they will have "seen" or "understood". The difference is not cosmetic: it decides whether you can assess at all. "Understand the notion of an objective" cannot be observed. "Write an objective containing an action verb" can be observed, corrected and graded.

    The test is simple: if you cannot picture the exercise that would prove the objective reached, it is badly written.

    4. The through-line

    A through-line is a case, a project or a scenario that runs across the whole course. It answers in advance the question the learner asks at every chapter: "what will this be used for?". This demo course has one: you are building a course while reading a course about building one.

    Key points

    • Three decisions, in order: for whom, to do what, around what. Each constrains the next.
    • An audience is described by its constraints, not by its degree title.
    • An objective is verifiable or it is nothing: if no exercise can prove it, it is badly written.
    • The through-line answers "what is this for" before the learner asks.
