    🧱 Chapter 2 ⏱ ~25 min · reading and activities

    Structuring the content

    Scoping settled for whom and to do what. What remains is deciding in which order, and in what pieces. This is where the difference is made between a course people finish and one they abandon at chapter three.

    🎯 Chapter objectives

    • Break a chapter into sequences that fit real attention spans.
    • Order notions by what they assume known, not by their importance.
    • Balance theory and practice without letting one crowd out the other.

    1. From syllabus to sequences

    A chapter is not a unit of learning: it is a unit of storage. What the learner actually goes through are sequences: blocks that start, end, and leave something behind.

    A useful sequence holds three parts: a question that motivates it, content that answers it, and a check that proves it landed. Remove the question and the learner does not know why they are reading. Remove the check and nobody knows whether it worked, not even them.

    2. Thinking in micro-learning

    Breaking up is not chopping up. A five-minute sequence that does not stand on its own is not micro-learning: it is a chapter cut in half. The test is simple: if the learner stops there, do they leave with something usable?

    It is also a format decision. A short sequence can be read on a commute; a forty-minute one assumes a desk and a closed door. The audience profile written in chapter 1 settles this for you: re-read it rather than deciding on instinct.

    3. Building a progression

    The most common mistake is ordering notions by importance: starting with what matters most. But a learner can only take on a notion if they already hold the ones it assumes. The right order is that of prerequisites, not of importance.

    A method that works: list your notions, then draw an arrow from each to those it assumes. Notions with no incoming arrow are your starting points. If you end up with a cycle (A assumes B which assumes A), then one of the two is badly cut.

    4. Balancing theory and practice

    An entirely theoretical course produces learners who can recite. An entirely practical one produces learners who can repeat, and nothing more: as soon as the case changes, they are lost. The balance is not a matter of proportions but of alternation: a notion, its application, then the next notion.

    The through-line acts as a guardrail here. If a sequence advances neither understanding nor the deliverable, it advances nothing at all, and it is the one to cut on the day a session is taken away from you.

    5. Review

    Flip the cards, then answer without looking at your notes.

    🔑

    Key points

    • A sequence has three parts: a question, content, a check. One is always missing, and it is almost always the third.
    • Breaking up is not chopping up: a short sequence must stand on its own.
    • The right order is that of prerequisites, not of importance.
    • Alternate rather than apportion: a notion, its application, the next one.
